The 1:40 rule is a theoretical idealisation, not a Gaza prediction

Fresh water is about 2.5% lighter than seawater, so it floats on it. Balance the pressure where the two meet and the theoretical depth of the interface follows from the height of the freshwater head.

z = h × ρf ÷ (ρs − ρf)

Here h is the freshwater head above sea level, z is the theoretical interface depth below it, ρf is fresh water at 1.000 g/cm³ and ρs is seawater at about 1.025 g/cm³. With those values z is 40 times h. This is the Ghyben-Herzberg relation (Post, Houben and van Engelen 2018). It describes an idealised, static balance. It is not a measurement or a forecast for any real aquifer, Gaza's included.

It assumes a sharp, static interface. Real aquifers have a mixing zone from metres to kilometres wide (Werner et al. 2013, USGS Circular 1262), and the ratio runs from about 1:33 to 1:50 as seawater density goes from 1.020 to 1.030. Interface depths in Gaza need field data and calibrated models, such as those cited below.

Seawater intrusion is one of several sources of salt in Gaza's groundwater, and no study ranks them

Not all salty groundwater is seawater intrusion. Brackish inflow from the east and deep brine are different processes, and relict water, brines and dissolved evaporites also count (Werner et al. 2013). Wastewater and fertiliser are mainly a nitrate problem, and the sources reviewed give no share of salinity for them. Select a source to highlight it.

Eastern inflow comes first

Weinthal et al. (2005) use boron, strontium and oxygen and hydrogen isotopes to argue that natural saline groundwater flowing in from the east is the primary source. Seawater is secondary and limited to the western margin.

Their data end around 2005.

Intrusion has grown since 2000

Mushtaha and Walraevens (2023) mapped chloride for 1972 to 2021. Before 2000 supply wells sat far from the sea. Afterwards some stood within 2 km of the coast, and intrusion followed.

They also map lateral inflow and upconing.

The two readings can both hold. They cover different periods, zones and sub-aquifers. No study splits the salinity between the sources, so intrusion is best described as one process among several, likely dominant on the western margin and in deep sub-aquifers since about 2000. That is an interpretation, not a measured share.

How fast does the wedge move?

Sarsak and Almasri (2013) ran a SEAWAT model of northern Gaza with projected sea-level rise. They estimate seawater moving inland at about 65 m a year now, 35 m a year in a best case and about 80 m a year by 2035 in a low-recharge worst case.

What the Gaza case shows

Head sets depth

When pumping runs roughly three to four times the sustainable yield and heads sit several to more than 15 m below sea level, the head that holds seawater back is lost. How far and how fast seawater advances then depends on time and aquifer geometry. The 1:40 relation is a guide to that balance, not a prediction.

Seawater is one source among several

Eastern inflow and deep brine add salt inland. Nitrate from sewage and fertiliser is a separate contamination problem, not a quantified source of salinity, and protecting the aquifer from the sea would not fix it.

Recovery is slow

Model results for Gaza (Qahman and Larabi; Selmi) show heads rising with less pumping and more recharge over 17 to 20 years. Those runs describe water levels, not salinity. In the Netherlands, salinity lags can exceed 100 years in some aquifers (Werner et al. 2013), and a 2012 UN-linked outlook said decades.

Unverified in the sources

The Palestinian Water Authority target of cutting abstraction from 180 to 70 MCM/yr by 2032 appears only in a secondary source. The "unusable by 2016, irreversible by 2020" projection comes from a news-service summary of a UN report. No source reviewed gives a quantified recovery time for Gaza groundwater quality.

Less pumping is not the same as recovery Scientific interpretation

Reducing abstraction does not mean the aquifer recovers straight away. Hydraulic recovery, the rise of groundwater levels, and water-quality recovery, the fall of salinity and contaminants, are separate outcomes. Levels can improve while the salt already in the aquifer stays, and a 2019 laboratory and numerical study shows seawater can keep advancing after pumping stops. Recovery in Gaza should be judged on both measures, and the sources reviewed quantify neither for the present day.
